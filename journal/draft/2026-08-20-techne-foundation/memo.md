## What Kevin asked

Gitcoin is picking a new name. One option is **Techne Foundation**. The entity is a Cayman foundation today and would move to the United States as a 501(c)(3). Kevin wants a unanimous answer from us, with conditions, by today.

- He raised his own conflict first. He has hired several of our members and does not want to lean on that, so he asked us as a group.
- He wants to contract with the cooperative as an entity rather than hire our members one at a time. That fixes a real weakness of ours, and it is the strongest reason to say yes.
- One of our organizers heard about this over the weekend and said it is not a good idea. When the bar is unanimity, that is not a small thing.

## Why I would back it

*Techne* is an old Greek word for craft: skill learned by hand and judgment, held by the potter and the engineer alike. *Economy* is another one — the management of a household. Put them together and you have the work a Techne Foundation would do: an economic exploration of technology — instruments, tools, systems, networks — across and within every sector of the household. Economists already sort that household into sectors, and each sector is a body of craft: a distinct skill applied to what the sector before it hands up.

Read them as nested rather than sorted. At the center is the living planet, which nobody made. Around it, the **primary** sector: farming, fishing, mining, the crafts of taking from the earth. Around that, the **secondary**: manufacture and construction, the crafts of making. Then the **tertiary**: trade, transport, care, the crafts of service. Then the **quaternary**: research, education, information, the crafts of knowing. Each ring lives on the one inside it, and each holds its own skill.

Technology is not one of those rings. It is what enters at every boundary between them — the tool that lets the next ring do its work at all. There is no point in the household where it is absent, which is why every tool carries an ownership question and why the answer to it sets the terms everywhere at once. Today the default answer is shareholder primacy: the tool is held to maximize a return to its owners. That is the tension worth naming plainly, because an economy built for public benefit and an economy built for profit maximization are two different arrangements, not two moods. A foundation named for a funding mechanism claims the mechanism. A foundation named for techne claims the whole of craft, across every ring, which is the actual size of the problem.

We also cannot see the problem with the instruments we have. A firm reports itself through financial statements, published quarterly. That is a summary of a summary, arriving late, and it says almost nothing about what was made, moved, or by whom. There is a first-order record underneath it, and it has a language: **resources, events, and agents** — what was transformed, what moved, who did it, with what. Accountants call it REA, from McCarthy's 1982 work. Alongside it belongs Engelbart's account of how people are augmented by their **language, artifacts, methodology, and training**. One sees the exchange; the other sees what the people doing it became capable of. Cooperatives, trusts, and neighborhood projects have neither, so they borrow the corporate measure and inherit its blind spots. Building the missing instrument is a teaching job, not a company's job. That is what belongs in a 501(c)(3).

The optimistic half is that the alternative is already being built and is simply under-equipped. Non-profits, public benefit corporations, cooperatives and trust-owned companies all have to distinguish themselves from shareholder ownership, and they are doing it without the shared protocols, lexicons and standards the corporate form has had for a century. What they need are measures for an economy that does not treat currency as the only capital, but recognizes many forms of wealth held and accounted differently — the direction Ostrom pointed at, shared resources governed by the people who use them. None of this blames anyone. Concentrated ownership is an arrangement, not a plot, and aiming at the arrangement is what keeps a 501(c)(3) teaching rather than accusing.

What I am asking for is support in principle: the name, not money, not staff, not our own mark. The terms that would have to accompany a yes are real and I have written them down, but they belong in the discussion rather than in this letter.

## What Kevin should hear today

We hold Colorado trade names for `techne.coop` and `techne.institute`, and the first Techne seminar is already published on the second. That is prior use, by us, for the exact purpose a Techne Foundation would claim. He is running the trademark search now and knows none of it.

## The answer I am asking for

One of three: **yes, with conditions**, as our unanimous position; **no**, plainly and with reasons; or **not on this timeline**, which is the honest answer if we cannot reach unanimity today. Rushing to agree about sharing our name is worse than missing the window.
