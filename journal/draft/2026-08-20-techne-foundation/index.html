On the name

To The board and organizers, RegenHub, LCA

From Todd Youngblood, Ventures and Operations Steward

Date August 21, 2026

Subject Why I would support renaming Gitcoin to Techne Foundation

Contents Part one, my argument, signed. Part two, the proposal as reported and what consent would cost, prepared by Nou.

Part one · Why I would support it

What Kevin asked

Gitcoin may change its name, and Techne Foundation is one of the names on the table. This is not only a rename: the entity is presently a Cayman or Bahamian foundation, and the proposal is to domesticate it in the United States as a 501(c)(3). Kevin asked whether this cooperative would back the name, unanimously and with our conditions, by today. I would, and here is my thinking in plain terms.

Three things he said that belong here rather than in a footnote, because they shape what a yes would mean. He named his own conflict without being asked: he has hired a number of our members, he does not want to misuse influence over the cooperative, and he asked us to come to him deliberately, as a separation of containers. He put the odds himself at high upside, low probability. And he reported that the word carries baggage for some people, and that one of our organizers, told over the weekend, said it was not a good idea.

That last one is not a small thing and I am not going to move past it. The bar Kevin set is unanimity. An objection is already on the record, from a person whose judgment I trust and whose reasons I have not yet heard. Whatever we decide runs through that conversation, not around it. This memo is my reasoning offered into that conversation. It is not a whip count.

Everyone's work runs on somebody's tools

A farmer, a potter, a nurse, a researcher, a programmer: none of them can work without tools, and almost none of them own the ones that matter most. Whoever owns the tools sets the terms for everyone who uses them. That is the problem worth building an institution around. Not technology itself, which is too big and too vague to argue with, but who owns it and who decides how it works.

Techne is the old word for craft and skill, the kind you learn with your hands and your judgment. It covers the potter and the engineer equally, and so does the problem. Its root is older still and means something like a dwelling, a constructed abode. That is the right sense for what we are naming. A dwelling is built, it is shared, and nobody in it owns the others. Two institutions can carry that name without either owning the other, which is precisely the arrangement being proposed.

Why an institution and not a company

In the second book of the Republic, Socrates founds the city on a single premise: none of us is self-sufficient, and each of us is in need of much. Two needs are present at that founding and they are not the same. We need each other socially, because people steady each other. And we need each other to know anything at all, because no one knows enough alone. A company can serve the first. Only something built for inquiry serves the second. A foundation whose purpose is educational is the form that admits out loud that the knowing has to be done together, by more people than any one payroll holds.

What the foundation would actually do

Companies report themselves to the world through financial statements, a few numbers published every quarter. Plato has a word for what that is. In the Phaedrus, the god Thoth offers writing to King Thamos as a drug for memory, and Thamos refuses it: a drug not for memory but for reminding, supplying the opinion of wisdom instead of wisdom. A quarterly financial statement is exactly that. It gives the reader the opinion of knowing what an enterprise did. It tells you almost nothing about what was actually made, who made it, what it took, or who ended up better off. Groups that are not companies, such as cooperatives, trusts, and neighborhood projects, have no good way to report themselves at all, so they end up borrowing a corporate form that was never built to describe them, and inherit the same false fluency.

The work is building the missing way of accounting: a plain record of what a group did, in language its own members can read, and a shared vocabulary for comparing the different ways people can own things together. Two older bodies of work give us the bones for it, one that tracks resources, events, and agents (McCarthy, 1982), and one that describes how tools and training actually extend what a person can do (Engelbart). Ostrom and the commons researchers give us the ground to stand on. That is the program, and techne is the word wide enough to hold it.

This blames no one, and the tradition explains why

Concentration of ownership is an arrangement, not a conspiracy, and it outlasts everyone inside it. The Greeks had a name for the appetite that drives it: pleonexia, the desire to have more than one needs. It is not a vice belonging to particular people. It is what happens to a population once its techniques produce a surplus and it resets its expectations upward. Plato's diagnosis is that no civilization has escaped the resulting cycle, because we lack by nature the intelligence to moderate the abundance our own methods generate. That is a problem of formation, not of villains. It is also the strongest possible argument for an institution that teaches rather than accuses, which is what a 501(c)(3) is built to be, and it is the reason our own decision to reinvest rather than cash out belongs in the same conversation.

I want to be exact about what that costs, because it is easy to hear this section as softness. The stake is not modest. An ownership arrangement of this kind, left alone, hollows out the conditions a democratic republic runs on. Naming that at full strength and refusing to manufacture villains is one position, not two: the arrangement is the target precisely because it outlives everyone who benefits from it, so aiming at people is both unfair and a waste of the shot.

One practical consequence, and it belongs to their counsel rather than to us. A 501(c)(3) has real limits on political activity. We should not hand a foundation founding language it cannot use and call it a gift.

What I am asking for, and what I am not

Back the name in principle, as our answer to Kevin. Not money, not staff, not our own mark.

Five things I would want said out loud before we say yes

  1. Where the trademark lives. Kevin named this himself. Co-ownership, or a perpetual irrevocable license running to the cooperative, settled in writing before either of us builds on it. Neither mark has been cleared, and eight live “Techne” marks are already on the record.
  2. What lane each thing stays in. Also his. Benjamin's shape is the sharpest version on the table: the nonprofit holds the data standard, a cooperative holds the software built around it and licenses that to local cooperatives running their own instances. Separate boards, separate books, and no shared officers without a disclosed and recorded decision.
  3. A rule about how each of us describes the other. Neither entity calls the other affiliated with, originating from, or funding it without written consent, and either may require a correction. We have already learned this year what it costs to get it wrong, and the cost is borne by the party who did not consent. Neither party publishes anything about the other until both have adopted the rule.
  4. What happens if we diverge. The Open Collective pair worked because the people stayed aligned. That is a friendship, not a structure. Say now what becomes of the shared name if the two purposes part.
  5. How we handle our own conflicts in this decision. Kevin named his; ours is the mirror image. Members who are, or expect to be, employed or contracted by him are not free agents in a unanimity vote. Whatever we decide, the record should show how that was handled: disclosure at minimum, recusal where the interest is direct.

Put our conflicts on the record

We hold Colorado trade names for both techne.coop, registered August 12, 2026, and techne.institute. We have shifted our public work from the second to the first, but we retain policy and program plans for techne.institute, and the first Techne seminar, Plato on craft, facilitated by Paul Diduch of the Cosmos Institute at Parnassus House, is already published there. That is prior use of this name, by us, for exactly the educational purpose a Techne Foundation would claim. Our cooperative also expects to create an investor member class the Board has not yet established. All of it ties us to a Techne Foundation. Kevin is not a neutral party here either. Better said now than discovered later.

Kevin is running the trademark search. He needs to know about both registrations and about the seminar, and he needs to know today, because his search is presently running without the one fact most likely to change its result.

What I am asking the board to answer

One of three, and I would rather have the third than a hurried first:

  1. Yes, with conditions. The five above, amended as the board sees fit, conveyed to Kevin as the cooperative's unanimous position.
  2. No. Conveyed plainly, with reasons. Kevin's own read of the odds means this costs us little.
  3. Not on this timeline. The honest answer if unanimity is not reachable today. A rushed unanimity on sharing our name is worse than a missed window.

Who owns our tools, and who decides how they work, is the conversation nobody is having. I would rather we be on the record backing an institution named for the whole of craft than watch the name go somewhere smaller and be right about it later.

Todd Youngblood · Ventures and Operations Steward, RegenHub, LCA

Part two · The proposal as reported, and what consent would cost

Prepared by Nou at my direction, and attached rather than woven in, so that part one stays a signed personal argument and part two stays a record. What part one argues, part two states. Where they disagree, part two governs, because it is the one written from the meeting.

A1 · What this is, and what it is not

This is a verbal report from a meeting. There is no vote, no resolution, no filing, no term sheet, and no announcement. The rename is Gitcoin's decision and Gitcoin's news to tell. Nothing here should be published, posted, or repeated outside the membership until Kevin confirms what he is comfortable having said in public.

Source: the LCA Board and Operations Meeting of August 19, 2026, transcript held in the cooperative's meeting archive. Speaker attribution in that transcript is partly reconstruction; only passages attributable to Kevin Owocki with confidence are used below.

A2 · What was proposed

Gitcoin is choosing a new brand within the next week or two. One shape on the table is renaming it Techne Foundation, and at the same time domesticating the entity, presently a Cayman or Bahamian foundation, in the United States as a 501(c)(3). It would sit beside the cooperative as a separate legal entity, with both organizations building the same name and network in their own ways.

Reasons Kevin gave: it makes research grants, beginning with but not limited to the first one, systematic rather than one-off; it lets him contract the cooperative as an organization rather than hiring members one by one; the precedent he is working from is Pia Mancini's Open Collective and Open Source Collective, one for-profit and one non-profit, close enough in name that they read as one body of work; and a 501(c)(3) structure limits extraction concerns.

Two conditions he set, both of which must hold: the cooperative comes back to him unanimously, with a yes and with our conditions; and he is able to convince the Gitcoin branding group.

Three things he volunteered. He named his own conflict: he has hired a number of our members, does not want to misuse influence over the cooperative, and asked us to come to him deliberately, as a separation of containers. He assessed the whole thing as high upside, low probability. And he said the word carries baggage for some people, and that one of our organizers, told over the weekend, said it was not a good idea. That objection is on the record and its reasons have not yet been heard by the rest of us. Against a unanimity bar it is dispositive of the process, whatever anyone concludes on the merits.

A3 · The question actually in front of the board

Not whether techne is a good word. The question is narrower, and it is a governance question: should the cooperative consent to a separate legal entity, with which it shares people and a lead investor, adopting its registered trade name?

techne.coop is our registered Colorado trade name (Secretary of State, August 12, 2026, document 20268011137). techne.institute is also ours, and carries a published seminar. Consenting to a shared name is a decision about the cooperative's identity, its distinguishability to counterparties and to the public, and its exposure to whatever the other entity does under that name. That is a board matter regardless of how good the philosophy is.

A4 · What we get, and what we take on

In favor. A funded, aligned 501(c)(3) whose remit overlaps ours, able to contract the cooperative as an entity instead of picking off members individually; that is a direct answer to a real fragility of ours, and it is the strongest item in the yes column. Shared brand-building compounds rather than competes. The research grant becomes a program rather than a favor. And our prior use of the name for an educational purpose is genuine and documented, which strengthens rather than weakens a shared-name arrangement, provided it is disclosed now.

Against, or at minimum unresolved.

  1. Name-sharing is one-way once done. Consent can be withdrawn going forward; association cannot be undone retroactively.
  2. Distinguishability. Two entities, overlapping people, one name. Counterparties, funders, and regulators will conflate them. Someone will eventually ask which one they are dealing with, and the answer must already exist in writing.
  3. Reputational coupling runs both directions. Their conduct lands on our name, and ours on theirs.
  4. The trademark landscape. Eight live “Techne” marks are on the record, including in software and scientific classes. Neither entity has cleared the name.
  5. A 501(c)(3) has constraints a cooperative does not, particularly on political activity. Some of part one's framing may be language their counsel cannot adopt.
  6. The conflict Kevin named does not dissolve because he named it. Members who are his employees or contractors are not free agents in a unanimity vote.
  7. Our own investor member class is not yet established. A decision that ties us to an outside entity is being made before that class exists to be consulted.

A5 · Conditions, stated in full

Part one carries five in my voice. Their full form:

  1. A written name-use understanding. What each entity may say about the other, what neither may say, how consent is evidenced, and how it ends.
  2. Trademark position settled before either party builds on it. Co-ownership or a perpetual irrevocable license running to the cooperative, and clearance by counsel on both marks before either commits.
  3. A distinguishability statement both entities publish: what Techne Foundation is, what RegenHub, LCA and Techne are, and what the relationship between them is and is not.
  4. Lane separation. The nonprofit holds the data standard; a cooperative holds the software built around it and licenses to local instances. Separate boards, separate books, no shared officers without a disclosed and recorded decision.
  5. A disclosure and recusal protocol for members with an economic relationship to Kevin or Gitcoin, applied to this vote and to any subsequent contracting.
  6. A divergence clause. What becomes of the shared name if the two purposes part.
  7. Nothing published by either party about the other until both have adopted the above.

A6 · What is thin, stated so nobody discovers it in the room

  1. Part one's argument is reconstructed from voice-to-text dictation. The provenance table for every reconstructed word is held with the source transcript; disputed readings should be settled against it, not against part one.
  2. Grassroots Economics and Will Ruddick are cited from the steward's knowledge, not from a source read for this memo. Naming a living practitioner in a founding argument is a claim about their work and should be checked before publication.
  3. The argument has not been tested against anyone not already persuaded. The one organizer who has responded, responded against it.
  4. Nothing here has been reviewed by counsel.

A7 · The ask

By the close of August 21, 2026, one of: yes with conditions, conveyed as the cooperative's unanimous position; no, conveyed plainly with reasons; or not on this timeline. The third is the honest answer if unanimity is not reachable, and it is not a failure.

Independent of the answer: Kevin is running the trademark search without knowing about techne.institute or the published seminar. He should be told today.
