The record

Where the cooperative stands in its own governance: which of two modes it is in, what each instrument (a governing document such as the Articles or the Bylaws) is and who may change it, what the record can and cannot hold, and what is still missing. This page reads the published instruments back to you. Nothing here runs, nothing here is adopted, and nothing you do on this page changes anything.

What this page is

A plain reading of the cooperative's governing documents, gathered in one place. It answers three questions: what state the cooperative is in right now, which document controls what and who may change it, and what this record still cannot do.

What you can do here

Read. There is no form and no button on this page, and nothing you do here changes the record. To read the documents themselves, go to the legal shelf. To see mistakes found after publication, go to Corrections and open readings. If this page disagrees with a document on the shelf, believe the document: this page is the one that is wrong.

The two modes

At any moment the cooperative is in one of two conditions: between the meetings, or in session (a meeting is actually open and running). Almost all of its life is spent in the first. Naming that plainly is the point: the ordinary condition is not a gap between the interesting parts.

Where we are

Between the meetings

No meeting is open. The governing documents cannot change.
What you can do
  • Say whether you will attend a coming meeting
  • Set the agenda, meaning the order of business
  • Give notice of a motion you want taken up
  • Record an interim act: something done between meetings under a standing authority the board granted in advance
  • Adopt the minutes of the meeting that just closed
  • Open the meeting. Get a quorum first: the minimum number of people who must be present for the meeting to count.
What you cannot do
  • Move, second, amend or withdraw a motion. Wait for a meeting to open.
  • Call the question, put it to a vote, cast a vote, or settle it
  • Change a governing document without a meeting. There is no authority that reaches one.

In session

A meeting is open. This body may change only its own documents.
What you can do
  • Everything the floor allows: move, second, amend, call the question, put it to a vote, cast, and settle it
  • Raise a point of order, meaning an objection that the rules are not being followed
  • Table a motion, meaning set it aside without deciding it
  • Adjourn. Close any open ballot first.
What you cannot do
  • Record an interim act. In session a body acts by motion instead.
  • Switch which body is meeting
  • Change a document the other body controls

The cooperative has been between the meetings for its whole existence, because no meeting of this Board has ever been opened on the record. If you want to know how the first one is meant to run, read The first meeting.

Every governing document, who may change it, and where it stands today. Each body may touch only its own documents. If the Board tries to change a document that belongs to the members, the attempt is refused, and the refusal tells you whose document it is instead of just failing. What this record cannot hold

These are not rules that someone might break. They are things the record simply cannot do, which is a stronger guarantee: a rule can be broken quietly, and a missing capability cannot.

A minute that reports what someone said
The record has no slot for what anyone said. Discussion is opened, and nothing spoken in it is written down. This is a missing capability, not a refusal, which is why an audio recording or a transcript counts for nothing here: there is nowhere to file it.
A record that certifies itself
Draft minutes assemble themselves from the acts that were recorded, but they stay a draft until a person votes to adopt them. Only a human can adopt them.
An act that erases an earlier one
Withdrawing or tabling a motion ends it; it does not delete it. Every motion that ever stood is still visible afterwards. To fix a mistake, add a new act that corrects it. You cannot edit the old one.
A quiet change to the governing documents between meetings
No delegated authority reaches a governing document. They change only when a meeting is open and a body votes. This is the governance twin of the treasury rule: no document, agent, or piece of software holds a key to the money.
A saved quorum figure that drifts from who is actually in the room
Quorum is always a fresh count, never a saved number. It is counted from who is present, every time it is asked for.
What is missing

A record that shows its holes is worth more than one that looks complete. These are the gaps as they stand today. The tag on each one tells you its kind: absent means the record has no way to do this at all, open question means a decision has not been made yet, not yet specified means no rule has been written for it, and open reading means the existing record can be read more than one way and nobody has settled which reading is right.

The minute book is empty.open reading

No meeting of this Board has minutes, meaning a written record of who attended, what was moved, and how the vote went. The June 2026 board actions come to us in four accounts that disagree about the date and about whether anything was adopted at all, and none of them is a set of minutes: no attendance, no motions, no vote, no quorum. The unresolved reading is logged at DOC-01·08. Two things would close it. A director who was in the room states what was done, or the board adopts the current documents over again at a meeting that records all of those things.

Signing a document has no shape here.absent

The act that makes the Bylaws take effect is one officer's signature, not a vote. This model says the legal state moves only when a meeting is open and a body votes, so it cannot describe a signature at all. Giving that act a name is the first piece of work.

Seats are not offices.open question

The table above names four holders: members, board, steward, derived. The Bylaws name three officers instead, a President, a Secretary and a Treasurer, each with duties tied to particular documents, and steward is not an office in the Bylaws at all. Which of the two vocabularies an act should point to has not been decided.

Which majority, and of what?open question

A majority of the people present, or a majority of the votes actually cast? Those two numbers come apart the moment anyone abstains. The same question is already open on the board side: Bylaws § 3.13 requires a simple majority of all Directors, and nobody has settled whether that means a majority of the six seats the Bylaws authorise or a majority of the directors currently serving. With all six seats filled the two give the same answer, so the question is harmless today. Drop to five directors and it decides votes.

Nobody can object to something done between meetings.absent

A member who disagrees with an act taken under delegated authority has nowhere to say so. You could read the record, but you could not answer it.

There is only one level of privacy.open question

Every act is either held or open, with nothing in between: the substance is withheld and the fact that the act happened is public. Nothing separates board-only from member-only from public, and no rule says when a withheld item becomes readable. Bylaws § 18.1 leaves that to the board.

You have to be present to vote.not yet specified

There is no provision for a member who cannot attend: no way to vote ahead of time, and no way to send someone in your place. For a cooperative that means to be spread across places, that is a large silence.

Standing

Designed, not built. This page describes a governance section for the Common Information System, the cooperative's shared record. That section is proposed and has not been built: the underlying system today has no way to hold a motion, a ballot, a quorum count or a set of minutes. The design this page follows is a drawing. Nobody adopted it, and it carries no authority of its own. Where this page and a governing document differ, the document is right and this page owes you a correction.
