The community of practice.

A community of practice is a group of people who care about the same craft and get better at it by doing it near one another. This page says what that means here, in plain words.

01What it is

Craft is learned by making, near people who make.

Nobody learns a craft alone. You learn it beside people who already have it: watching how they hold the work, asking why they did it that way, being watched in turn. A community of practice is the old name for that arrangement, older than any school, and it is what this cooperative exists to hold.

The crafts here are many. Software and gardens, teaching and neighboring, writing and the making of institutions themselves. What joins them is not a shared discipline but a shared orientation: care for how the thing is made, and the conviction that technology, in the oldest sense of the word, is skill, art, and technique before it is any particular machine.

The drafted Bylaws subscribe to the cooperative principle of education, training, and information: a cooperative teaches its members and informs the public about what it is. Those Bylaws are in review and not executed, so the subscription is a draft's promise; this page, and the practice it describes, is the promise already being kept.

02The rhythms

How the practice actually happens.

Gatherings

The room fills on a rhythm: open evenings, member gatherings, the occasional launch. Most people meet the community at a gathering before they meet it any other way.

Courses

Structured learning, held in the space and grown from it: a craft taught over weeks by the people who practice it, to whoever is ready to take it up.

Working aloud

The daily texture: work done where it can be seen, questions asked across the table, progress shared as it happens rather than presented when finished. The record this site keeps is the same habit at estate scale.

Learning beside experienced hands

Newcomers sit near people further along, and belonging grows with skill. Nothing formal has to happen for the teaching to happen; proximity does most of it.

03The test

Orientation over output.

The formation meetings worked out, by cases, what makes someone part of this. It is not what you produce. Someone collecting a paycheck with no curiosity about what the community is building is not here, however productive; a student with no path figured out but genuine excitement is. Nobody has to be doing explicitly impact-oriented work. What matters is a current of community care running underneath whatever the vocation is.

The room named this orientation over output, and it remains the membership test the community actually uses: not a measure of what you make, but a reading of which way you face.

The test was worked out in Meeting 4 of the formation series, September 19, 2025, and is kept with the rest of the formation vocabulary in the Commonplace.

04The rings

Belonging is concentric.

The cooperative draws one clean legal line: you have either signed a member agreement or you have not. The formation meetings were explicit that the legal boundary must not become the social one. The picture the room chose instead is rings.

the circles of belonging, drawn from the center out
  • A core of cooperative members, once the instruments open and members are admitted.
  • People who work here without joining the cooperative: a desk, the rhythms, none of the governance.
  • People who come around: gatherings, courses, the scene, the pleasure of the room.
  • The wider field of people who might one day, and are welcome long before they decide.

Growth means inviting people inward, not policing the edge. Meeting 4, September 19, 2025, in the Commonplace.
